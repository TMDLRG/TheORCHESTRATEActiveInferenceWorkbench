Step 2 · Pin it down

Risk is the pragmatic half (get what I want); ambiguity is the epistemic half (know where I stand). Now let’s make each one a number.

1 · The two halves as quantities

Write each half as something you can measure

Both are in nats, the units from Part I.

  • Risk = how far the outcomes you’d predict sit from the outcomes you prefer. It’s a KL divergence — the exact tool from Module 6: risk = KL( predicted outcomes ‖ preferred outcomes ). Zero when your predicted ending is what you want; grows as they diverge. (Exact and checkable.)
  • Ambiguity = how blurry your observations will be about the hidden state — the expected entropy of the likelihood: ambiguity = E_state[ H( observation | state ) ]. Small when an observation sharply tells you where you are; large when what you’ll see barely depends on the truth. (Illustrative values here, read from each action’s likelihood.)
G = risk + ambiguity — minimise it. Low risk and low ambiguity: the action gets you what you want and leaves you knowing where you stand.

▸ Go deeper — the canonical form derivation
For a policy π: G(π) = KL[ Q(o|π) ‖ C(o) ] + E_Q(s|π)[ H[P(o|s)] ] — i.e. risk + ambiguity, with C the preference prior over outcomes and P(o|s) the likelihood. Equivalent split: G = −E_Q[ln C(o)] (expected cost under preferences) − I(o;s) (expected information gain), up to constants — so minimising G maximises preference-satisfaction and information gain at once. The ambiguity values here (0.60, 0.05) are the expected likelihood entropies of each action’s observations — a near-state-blind sweater vs. a state-revealing stove, the same H = −Σ p ln p you used for bits/nats in Part I. (Risk and the KL are exact and checkable; the ambiguities are illustrative values read from each action’s likelihood.)
4 · Where this goes next

Now the engine is a number

G = risk + ambiguity, minimised — and you’ve seen the epistemic half overturn a decision reward alone would have gotten wrong. Explore/exploit isn’t a rule bolted on; it’s 0.60 versus 0.36 — the risk exact by hand, the ambiguity (and so the totals) read from each action’s likelihood, illustrative here.

One thing we’ve assumed: that a mind, having scored its options, simply takes the lowest. But how hard does it commit to the winner — bet everything on the best policy, or hedge across several? That “how sharply do I commit” is itself a quantity a mind sets and tunes. It’s precision, and it’s Module 15 — the last rung of Part II.
