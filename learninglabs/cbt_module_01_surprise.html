The one fence that never moves. “Surprise,” and later “free energy,” are functional quantities — numbers a model computes about its own predictions. They are not feelings, not awareness, not sentience. When we say a system “wants” low surprise, that is shorthand for “its dynamics move in the direction that lowers this number.” We keep that line bright on purpose.

0 · The question underneath

Why a book about minds begins with a feeling

Here is the question this whole course is quietly answering: how does a mind — yours, an animal’s, or the artificial one being built in this lab — make sense of a world it can never see directly?

You never touch the world. You get hints of it: light on your eyes, pressure on your skin, sound in your ears. From those hints a mind has to guess what’s out there, act on the guess, and not die of being wrong. The astonishing thing is that the entire job reduces to one feeling turned into a number, and then to one move repeated forever. The feeling is surprise. The move is keep surprise low.

So we start exactly where a mind starts: with the jolt of being wrong. No equations yet — just a feeling.

▶ Picture it kid
Surprise is the size of your flinch when reality taps you on the shoulder. A big flinch means “my picture of things was badly off.” A tiny flinch means “yep, saw that coming.”
1 · The stakes

What surprise is really for

Two mornings. Both end with you soaked to the skin.

  • Monday: the app said 10% chance of rain. It poured.
  • Tuesday: the app said 90% chance of rain. It poured.

Same wet shirt both days. But Monday you felt it — “you have got to be kidding me” — and Tuesday you just sighed and opened the umbrella you’d packed. Identical outcome, completely different jolt.

That jolt is surprise, and notice what it tracks. Not the weather (same both days). Not whether the outcome was good or bad (getting wet is annoying both days). It tracks how much your expectation had ruled the outcome out. A mind runs on predictions; surprise is how it finds out a prediction just failed. Staying un-surprised, over a lifetime, is understanding your world.

✎ Try it (one minute) equation · by hand
Rank these five jolts from smallest to biggest, by gut: (a) the 10%-rain soaking, (b) a famously-reliable friend showing up 20 minutes late, (c) a coin you called “heads” landing tails, (d) your lottery ticket actually winning, (e) your usual bus arriving on time. Now say in one sentence what your ranking is really tracking. It isn’t good-vs-bad, and it isn’t how much happened — it’s how little probability you’d parked on each thing.
2 · The hinge

Surprise lives in your model, not in the world

Think of two separate jobs:

  • Reality’s job: decide what actually happens. It picked “rain.”
  • Your job: supply the probability you had parked on that outcome, beforehand.

Surprise is built from your number for the slot reality chose. Nothing else. And look what falls straight out: if you had been certain it would rain (probability 1) and it rained, your surprise should be zero — you can’t be surprised by what you already knew. Before we put any formula down, let’s agree on the shape any honest surprise number must have. Call the surprise of an outcome you gave probability p by the name S(p). It must obey three rules:

  1. S(1) = 0 — a sure thing happening is no surprise at all.
  2. S gets bigger as p gets smaller — rarer stings more, smoothly.
  3. S is never negative — surprise doesn’t run in reverse.

We haven’t chosen a formula. We’ve written down the rules a formula would have to satisfy.

3 · The one demand that does all the work

Putting a number on it — and why it has to be a logarithm

Flip two fair coins in two separate rooms. They land heads and heads. The two flips have nothing to do with each other, so the only honest thing is that the two shocks add. For two unrelated outcomes with probabilities p and q (whose joint probability is p × q, because that’s what “independent” means):

S(p × q) = S(p) + S(q)

Call that the demand: independent surprises add. A lot of innocent-looking formulas seem fine until you test them against this one demand. So don’t trust any of them yet — put them on the bench and try to break them.

Break the impostors

Three candidate surprise rules are “standing.” Test each event-pair against the demand S(p×q)=S(p)+S(q). A rule is eliminated the moment it breaks additivity on any pair. Only one can survive — find out which, and why.

Test a pair:

Only −log survives. That is not luck. A logarithm is the one function in all of mathematics whose defining identity is “turn multiplying into adding”: log(p×q) = log(p) + log(q), always. The demand you insisted on is that identity. Everything else had to die.

What survives is the logarithm. Two small fixes finish the job. A log of a number below 1 is negative, but surprise must be positive — so put a minus sign in front. And log(1) = 0 automatically, which hands us rule 1 for free. So:

surprise of an outcome = −log( the probability you gave it )
written S(p) = −log p · its proper name is surprisal

You didn’t swallow that formula. You watched two reasonable alternatives die against a rule you insisted on, and watched the logarithm be the thing standing when the dust cleared.

▶ Picture it — what a logarithm even is kid
Forget “logarithm” the scary word. −log₂ p just counts how many times you have to halve 1 to get down to p. Halve 1 → ½ (that’s 1). Halve again → ¼ (that’s 2). Again → ⅛ (that’s 3). So the surprise of something with probability ⅛ is 3 “halvings.” Rarer things sit further down the ladder, so they score higher. Move the ladder below and watch it.

The halving ladder

Set a probability and see −log₂ p as the number of halvings from 1 down to p. This is why it’s a logarithm: halvings add, which is exactly the demand.

Jump to:

The unit. If you count in halvings (log base 2), surprise comes out in bits. If you use the “natural” log (base e, the one on your calculator marked ln), it comes out in nats. Same idea, different ruler. The system in this lab works in nats.

✓ Check it yourself (two minutes) equation · by hand
  • Fair coin lands heads, p = ½: −log₂(½) = 1 bit. One honest yes/no question’s worth of “huh.”
  • Two coins both heads, p = ¼: −log₂(¼) = 2 bits — exactly double one coin. The demand, working in your hands.
  • Fair die shows a 4, p = ⅙: −log₂(⅙) ≈ 2.58 bits — rarer than one coin, less than two.

▸ Go deeper — the functional equation derivation
We want S : (0,1] → [0,∞), continuous and decreasing, with S(pq) = S(p) + S(q) for all p, q. That functional equation (a cousin of Cauchy’s) has exactly one family of solutions, S(p) = −k·log p for a positive constant k: continuity rules out the pathological non-measurable solutions, monotonicity fixes the sign, and the base you pick is the constant k — it only sets the unit (bits, nats, or “bans” in base 10). Nothing else survives. This is why information theory, thermodynamics, and active inference all speak in logs: it is forced, not chosen.
⬡ See it in UNI the system
−ln p is the real quantity the system reports, under the name surprisal. The organism you can watch in the other labs is doing exactly this all day: it predicts its next sensory hint, reality delivers one, and when the two disagree it adjusts its beliefs to make that kind of contradiction less surprising next time — “rolling its beliefs downhill,” where downhill means toward less surprise. That is the move the rest of the course earns the right to say precisely.

5 · Where this goes next

Stand back and see what you have

You can now put an exact, checkable number on the surprise of a single outcome, under probabilities you trust. That’s a real tool, and the rest of the theory is built on it.

But look at the soft spot. Those probabilities came from a model — your picture of how the world works — and your model is not the world. So two questions open up: what is the true surprise of what you actually saw, over all the causes that could have produced it (it needs a sum that, for anything as big as a brain, you can never actually do — the wall); and if you can’t compute your true surprise, how can a mind possibly minimize it? The answer to the second is the thing the book is named for: free energy — which will turn out to be a height, a belief a position, and “making sense of the world” a ball rolling downhill into a valley whose lowest point is a truth you couldn’t compute directly. That’s the next module. For now: you turned a feeling into a number, and earned every symbol in it.
